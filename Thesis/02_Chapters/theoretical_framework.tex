\section{Theoretical Framework}

    \subsection{Optical Digit}

        The \textit{Optical Digit} dataset is a classical benchmark in pattern recognition and classification algorithm evaluation, especially in the context of \textit{OCR} (Optical Character Recognition). 
        Its original purpose was to provide a simple, reproducible, and balanced testbed for comparing supervised learning models without the computational cost of high-resolution images. 
        In other words, it is a lightweight yet expressive \textit{benchmark} for studying the generalisation capacity of different approaches.

        It consists of 8×8-pixel greyscale images (as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:optical_digit}); each image represents a digit in the range \(0\)–\(9\). 
        The dataset contains a total of 5,620 images and maintains a class balance: each digit has the same number of samples. 
        This is a relevant feature, as avoiding class-frequency bias allows metrics (accuracy, F1, etc.) to more fairly reflect differences between models and preprocessing \textit{pipelines}.

        \begin{figure}[H]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=5cm]{optical_digit/four_optical_digit.png}
            \caption{Optical Digit Dataset}
            \label{fig:optical_digit}
        \end{figure}

        Thanks to its balance between simplicity and usefulness, \textit{Optical Digit} is employed in various areas of artificial intelligence, such as:
        
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Digit classification \cite{pechuan2015}. 
            \item Pattern recognition \cite{huerta2009}.
            \item Model comparison and benchmarking \cite{mercado2015}. 
            \item Training and teaching in supervised learning \cite{jacznik2016}.
        \end{enumerate}

        Its advantages include: (\textit{i}) very fast training and validation curves (ideal for iterating hyperparameters and architectures), (\textit{ii}) ease of experiment reproduction (minimal transformations and preprocessing), and (\textit{iii}) usefulness as a \textit{case study} for methodologies such as incremental learning, where it is relevant to observe the effect of introducing sequential data blocks without excessive computational cost.

    \subsection{Artificial Neural Networks}

        Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) are computational models inspired by the functioning of the human brain, designed to process information in a parallel and distributed manner. 
Each network consists of a set of simple processing units called \textit{artificial neurons}, interconnected through weighted links known as \textit{synaptic weights}. 
These weights represent the strength of the connection between neurons and determine the influence that one input exerts over another within the network. 
In this sense, the learning of an ANN essentially consists of the progressive adjustment of these weights to minimise the error between the generated and expected outputs. 

Analogous to neurophysiology, where synaptic connections are strengthened or weakened based on experience, ANNs learn by modifying the numerical weights of their connections through training algorithms. 
This adaptive capability gives neural networks the ability to recognise patterns, classify information, make predictions, and approximate complex functions without explicit programming.

The simplest neuron model—known as the \textit{perceptron}—is the fundamental unit of ANNs. 
Introduced by Rosenblatt in 1958, the perceptron aims to emulate the behaviour of a biological neuron by performing a weighted combination of its inputs, followed by an activation function that determines its output. 
Mathematically, this model can solve \textit{linearly separable} problems, in which data can be divided by a linear boundary in the input space.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=8cm]{neural_networks/multilayer_perceptron/ANN.jpg}
    \caption{Diagram of a basic artificial neural network.}
    \label{fig:nerural_network}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:nerural_network}, the symbol \(\Sigma\) represents the summation operator. 
Let \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\) be the input variables and \(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n\) the weights associated with each synaptic connection. 
The neuron first computes a linear combination of the inputs weighted by their respective weights and adds an additional term known as the \textit{bias} (\(b\)), which allows shifting the decision boundary with respect to the origin. 
The mathematical model of the neuron can be expressed as:

\[
    y = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i + b,
\]

where \(y\) represents the output of the neuron. 
The bias plays an essential role as it introduces flexibility to the model, allowing the adjustment of the decision boundary position without altering the weight values. 
Thus, a single neuron acts as a linear classifier, separating the input data through a hyperplane in the feature space. 

However, the limitations of the perceptron become evident when dealing with non-linear problems, where a single boundary is insufficient for correctly separating the classes. 
This restriction led to the development of architectures with multiple layers of neurons and non-linear activation functions, known as \textit{multilayer neural networks} (MLPs), which allow modelling more complex relationships between input and output variables.

A single neuron can solve tasks such as AND or OR, but not non-linear problems (for example, XOR). 
In these cases, architectures with multiple layers are required, supported by activation functions that introduce the necessary non-linearity to model complex decision surfaces.

\paragraph{Logic gates and linear separability in ANNs.}
Binary logic gates illustrate the relationship between linear separability and the representational capacity of an artificial neural network.

\begin{itemize}
    \item AND: produces 1 only when both inputs are 1. It is linearly separable; a perceptron can implement it, for example, with \(w_1 = w_2 = 1\) and \(b = -1.5\). The boundary \(x_1 + x_2 - 1.5 = 0\) correctly separates the only positive case \((1,1)\).
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.38\linewidth]{logic_gates/and.png}
    \caption{AND logic gate in the input plane.}
    \label{fig:logic_gate_and}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
    \item OR: produces 1 when at least one input is 1. It is also linearly separable; a perceptron can implement it with \(w_1 = w_2 = 1\) and \(b = -0.5\). The boundary \(x_1 + x_2 - 0.5 = 0\) separates the three positive cases \((1,0), (0,1), (1,1)\) from the negative one \((0,0)\).
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.38\linewidth]{logic_gates/or.png}
    \caption{OR logic gate in the input plane.}
    \label{fig:logic_gate_or}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
    \item XOR: produces 1 when exactly one of the inputs is 1. It is not linearly separable, as no line can simultaneously divide the positive cases \((1,0), (0,1)\) from the negative ones \((0,0), (1,1)\). To solve XOR, at least one hidden layer (MLP) is required, combining multiple linear boundaries into a non-linear decision surface.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=4cm]{logic_gates/xor.png} 
    \caption{XOR logic gate: example of a non-linearly separable problem.}
    \label{fig:xor_logic_gate}
\end{figure}

This relationship is fundamental in neural architecture design: when data are not separable by a hyperplane, a multilayer network with suitable activation functions can compose linear boundaries into non-linear shapes, thus correctly representing the problem (see Section~\ref{sec:redes_mlp}).

\subsubsection{Activation Function} \label{sec:activation}

Activation functions are the component that transforms a stacked set of linear transformations into a non-linear model. 
Without them, even multiple layers would be equivalent to a single linear transformation. 
In problems where the input–output relationship is inherently non-linear (as in most real-world phenomena), activation functions enable the curvature of the decision boundary and stabilise gradient flow during training \cite{renganathan2019}.

Common activation functions include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step Function:} \\
        Suitable for simple binary decisions (0/1). Its modern use is educational, as its non-differentiability complicates gradient-based training:
        \begin{figure}[H]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=5cm]{activation_functions/staggered.png}
            \caption{Step Function}
            \label{fig:step_function}
        \end{figure}
        \[
        f(x) = \left\{ \begin{array}{lr} 
        0 & : x < 0 \\
        1 & : x \ge 0 
        \end{array} \right.
        \]
    
    \item \textbf{Sigmoid Function:} \\
        Produces outputs in \([0,1]\) and allows a probabilistic interpretation (e.g., 0.8 \(\Rightarrow\) 80\%). It also smooths decisions and facilitates gradient computation:
        \begin{figure}[H]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=5cm]{activation_functions/sigmoid.png}
            \caption{Sigmoid Function}
            \label{fig:sigmoid_function}
        \end{figure}
        \[
        f(x) = \sigma(x) =  \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}.
        \]
        Its parameters are adjusted through backpropagation (see Section~\ref{sec:backpropagation}).
    
    \item \textbf{Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU):} \\
        The de facto standard in deep networks due to its efficiency and ability to mitigate the \textit{vanishing gradient} problem. 
        It activates positive values proportionally and nullifies negative ones, accelerating convergence:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textit{Vanishing gradient:} phenomenon where gradients become very small and early layers stop updating effectively.
        \end{itemize}
        \begin{figure}[H]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=6cm]{activation_functions/relu.png}
            \caption{ReLU Function}
            \label{fig:relu_function}
        \end{figure}
        \[
        f(x) = \left\{ \begin{array}{lr} 
        0 & : x < 0 \\
        x & : x \ge 0 
        \end{array} \right.
        \]
    
    \item \textbf{Softmax Function:} \\
        Used in the output layer for multi-class classification, converting activations into probabilities that sum to 1:
        \begin{figure}[H]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=6cm]{activation_functions/softmax.png}
            \caption{Softmax Function}
            \label{fig:softmax_function}
        \end{figure}
        \[
        f(z)_j = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K} e^{z_k}}.
        \]
    
    \item \textbf{Hyperbolic Tangent Function:} \\
        Similar to the sigmoid function but with range \([-1,1]\), useful for centring data around zero; in very deep networks, it may still suffer from vanishing gradients:
        \begin{figure}[H]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=6cm]{activation_functions/tanh.png}
            \caption{Hyperbolic Tangent Function}
            \label{fig:tanh_function}
        \end{figure}
        \[
        f(x) = \tanh(x) = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{e^x + e^{-x}}.
        \]
\end{itemize}

In summary, ANNs enable tackling non-linear relationships, learning complex input–output mappings, and obtaining models that are more robust to noise. 
Among the most widespread architectures are multilayer perceptrons and convolutional networks, described below.

\subsection{Multilayer Perceptron Neural Networks} \label{sec:redes_mlp}

Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) Neural Networks consist of an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. 
The hidden layers contain neurons with activation functions (see Section~\ref{sec:activation}), responsible for breaking linearity and building decision surfaces in higher-dimensional spaces. 
Thus, problems that a single neuron cannot solve (such as XOR) become tractable by combining multiple layers and activations.

Figure~\ref{fig:multilayer_perceptron} illustrates a 4-layer MLP: one input, two hidden, and one output. 
The input layer injects the variables; non-linear processing occurs in the hidden layers (and, in certain designs, also in the output). 
This modular structure facilitates adding depth or width according to the problem’s complexity.

\subsection{Backpropagation Algorithm} \label{sec:backpropagation}

The backpropagation algorithm allows the adjustment of the network parameters (weights and biases) based on the gradient of a loss function. 
It was key to extending the capability of the simple perceptron to deep networks capable of solving non-linear problems.

Operationally, the network first performs a forward propagation to compute the output and the error; the error is then propagated backwards (backpropagation) by applying the chain rule to obtain the partial derivatives with respect to each parameter. 
With these derivatives, weights and biases are updated through an optimiser (gradient descent or others). 
The process requires differentiable activation functions and an appropriate loss function \cite{Rojas1996}. 

The flow is summarised in two phases:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Feed-forward}: Propagation of inputs forwards to obtain outputs and identify the error.
    \item \textbf{Backpropagation}: Backward propagation of the error and parameter updates layer by layer.
\end{enumerate}

For a detailed description, see Appendix \ref{ap:backprop} (Sections \ref{ap:feed_forward} to \ref{ap:back_propagation}).

\subsection{Convolutional Neural Networks}

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are deep architectures specialised in data with spatial structure (images, visual sequences, local patterns). 
Their success relies on three complementary types of layers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Convolutional}: apply local filters that detect edges, textures, and shapes; they allow partial translational invariance.
    \item \textbf{Pooling}: reduce dimensionality and computational cost while preserving salient features.
    \item \textbf{Fully connected}: integrate the extracted information and perform classification.
\end{itemize}
This design has proven effective in computer vision, medical image diagnosis, robotics, and speech recognition, among other fields.

\subsection{Incremental Learning}

The constant increase in the amount of available data and the demand for systems capable of learning continuously have driven the development of Incremental Learning. 
In this approach, training examples are presented sequentially, and the model must adapt to new information without forgetting previously acquired knowledge. 
Unlike traditional supervised learning schemes—in which the entire dataset is processed simultaneously—incremental learning trains the model progressively, gradually integrating new patterns or tasks \cite{GiraudCarrier2000}.

This feature is essential in dynamic or non-stationary environments, where data change over time and it is not feasible to carry out full retraining at each update. 
In fields such as robotics, systems must constantly adapt to new scenarios, lighting conditions, textures, or human behaviours without forgetting prior information. 
Similarly, in applications such as cybersecurity, image processing, speech recognition, or recommender systems, the continuous data flow requires models capable of adjusting their knowledge in real time without degrading historical performance.

Incremental learning is directly inspired by the way human beings learn: people are able to incorporate new knowledge over time while retaining relevant information from past experiences. 
For neural networks, replicating this type of learning involves balancing two key properties: plasticity, which allows adaptation to new data, and stability, which preserves previously learned knowledge. 
This balance, known as the stability–plasticity dilemma, is one of the main challenges of incremental learning.

However, conventional neural networks exhibit a phenomenon called catastrophic forgetting, in which the incorporation of new tasks or classes leads to the loss of previously learned information. 
This occurs because the optimisation process adjusts the network’s weights to adapt to the new data, overwriting previous knowledge. 
As a result, the model “forgets” past tasks when faced with new training sets \cite{liu2015}.

To mitigate this problem, various strategies have been proposed within incremental learning, including:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regularisation-based methods:} impose constraints on the weights most important for previous tasks, preventing them from changing drastically during the learning of new tasks. Examples include Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) and Synaptic Intelligence (SI).
    
    \item \textbf{Replay methods:} store a small number of representative examples or generate synthetic data to recall prior knowledge while learning new tasks.
    
    \item \textbf{Parameter isolation methods:} reserve subsets of neurons or specific layers for each task, preventing direct interference between them.
\end{itemize}

The general objective of these techniques is to maintain the ability to learn continuously without retraining the model from scratch or permanently storing previous data. 
In this way, incremental learning is consolidated as a central paradigm of Continual Learning, enabling the development of more efficient, adaptive, and autonomous intelligent systems. 
In short, it seeks to bring artificial neural networks closer to human cognitive behaviour, where knowledge is built progressively and preserved over time.

\subsubsection{Incremental Learning Algorithms}

In general terms, an incremental learning algorithm seeks to maintain the ability to learn continuously without retraining the entire model from scratch. 
These algorithms are designed to operate under the premise that data are not presented statically or simultaneously, but as a sequence of experiences or information blocks that must be processed as they arrive. 
The central challenge is to update the model parameters without compromising previously acquired knowledge, avoiding catastrophic forgetting.

To fulfil this role, an incremental algorithm must meet the following essential criteria \cite{Deshmukh2013}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Learn and update with each new datum, labelled or unlabelled, without requiring a global retraining process.
    \item Preserve previously acquired knowledge, integrating new information without degrading prior learning.
    \item Avoid dependence on permanent access to the original data (learning without full storage of past examples).
    \item Detect and create new classes or clusters when the data introduce previously unseen patterns, as well as split or merge existing clusters according to the environment.
    \item Maintain a dynamic and adaptive nature in the face of conceptual or distributional changes in the data (concept drift).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{incremental_learning/incremental_learning_methods.png}
    \caption{Two traditional approaches to incremental learning.}
    \label{fig:incremental_learning_algorithm}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:incremental_learning_algorithm}, there are two fundamental historical approaches to incremental learning:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cumulative or sequential approach:} 
    each time a new data block \(D_j\) arrives, the model discards the previous hypothesis \(h_{j-1}\) and trains a new hypothesis \(h_j\) using all available data. 
    Although global accuracy improves, this method is computationally expensive and does not represent truly continuous learning.
    
    \item \textbf{Purely incremental approach:} 
    the model receives \(D_j\) and directly updates the parameters of the existing hypothesis \(h_{j-1}\), relying solely on the new information. 
    In some cases, voting mechanisms or hypothesis combinations are used to integrate changes stably between successive iterations.
\end{itemize}

In both cases, the main practical advantage of the incremental approach lies in the fact that the acquired knowledge is not stored as individual examples, but is encoded in the model parameters (weights, biases, or probabilities), thereby avoiding the storage of large volumes of historical data.

From a formal point of view, given a sequence of training examples \(e_1, e_2, \dots, e_s\), an incremental algorithm generates a series of hypotheses \(h_0, h_1, \dots, h_n\) such that each hypothesis \(h_{i+1}\) depends only on the previous hypothesis \(h_i\) and the current example \(e\) \cite{GiraudCarrier2000}. 
This principle enables adaptive and continuous learning, where the model “evolves” with each interaction.

A classic example of incremental learning is the COBWEB model \cite{fisher1987}, which performs hierarchical probabilistic categorisation. 
COBWEB analyses each incoming instance and decides whether to create a new category, update an existing one, or reorganise the hierarchy, based on a global utility measure. 
In this way, the system builds a knowledge tree that is updated as new data are received, without the need to restart the learning process.

As the field has advanced, various families of incremental algorithms have been proposed, classified according to their main strategy:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regularisation methods:} restrict changes to parameters most relevant to previous tasks, as in Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC), Synaptic Intelligence (SI), or Memory Aware Synapses (MAS).
    \item \textbf{Replay methods:} employ a small memory of examples or generative models to recall previous tasks during the training of new ones. Examples include iCaRL and Generative Replay.
    \item \textbf{Parameter isolation methods:} assign subsets of neurons or specific layers to different tasks, reducing interference between knowledge (Progressive Neural Networks, PackNet).
    \item \textbf{Hybrid methods:} combine regularisation, replay, and dynamic expansion to balance stability and plasticity in non-stationary contexts.
\end{itemize}

Within this line of research, the work of \cite{bullinaria2009} represents an important reference point, as it introduces an alternative approach based on dual memory: a short-term memory responsible for storing and consolidating recent information, and a long-term memory that preserves already stabilised knowledge. 
This mechanism is implemented through a duplication of synaptic weights (two copies of the same set of connections) that allows differentiated control of forgetting rate and learning speed.

The model proposed by Bullinaria employs incremental learning in which each data block updates only the short-term network; then, the consolidated information is gradually transferred to the long-term network, thus avoiding the direct overwriting of the main weights. 
This structure emulates, in a simplified manner, the memory consolidation processes observed in the human brain (hippocampus–neocortex), offering an efficient alternative to mitigate catastrophic forgetting without resorting to explicit storage of previous data.

In the present work, this principle is adopted and extended, exploring the possibility of using more than two sets of duplicated weights to analyse whether a higher degree of partitioning in synaptic memory can offer better stability and knowledge retention throughout the incremental learning process.

\subsubsection{Memory Mechanisms in Incremental Learning}

The human learning process combines multiple levels of memory that interact hierarchically. 
Inspired by this principle, incremental learning in neural networks seeks to emulate the distinction between short-term memory (recent and volatile information) and long-term memory (consolidated and stable knowledge). 

In the computational context, these mechanisms are represented by different storage structures or duplication of synaptic weights. 
Models such as that proposed by \cite{bullinaria2009} use two sets of weights: one for rapid updating (similar to the hippocampus) and another for slow consolidation (analogous to the neocortex). 
This architecture allows plasticity to be maintained during the learning of new patterns without compromising the stability of previously acquired knowledge.

The study of these interactions forms the basis for the design of the present work, which proposes extending the dual-memory approach towards a scheme of multiple retention levels, in order to evaluate its impact on information loss and the efficiency of incremental learning.

\subsubsection{Strategies to Mitigate Catastrophic Forgetting}

One of the greatest challenges in incremental learning is catastrophic forgetting, a phenomenon that occurs when a neural network loses part or all of the knowledge previously acquired when learning new tasks or incorporating recent data. 
This problem arises because the optimisation process adjusts the network’s weights to fit the new information, overwriting parameters that previously represented prior knowledge. 
Consequently, the model may show excellent performance on the most recent task but poor performance on earlier ones.

To mitigate this problem, the literature proposes different approaches that seek to balance two fundamental principles: plasticity (ability to adapt to new tasks) and stability (ability to retain previous knowledge), commonly referred to as the stability–plasticity dilemma. 
The main strategies used to counteract catastrophic forgetting in incremental learning are described below:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Regularisation-based methods:} \\
    These methods add constraints to the weight update process, preventing those that proved important for previous tasks from changing drastically during the learning of new ones. 
    A classic example is Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC), which computes a parameter-importance matrix and penalises modifications to them \cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}.
    Modern variants such as Synaptic Intelligence (SI) or Memory Aware Synapses (MAS) follow a similar principle: they maintain a “synaptic memory” of the parameters most relevant to previous tasks, reinforcing their preservation.

    \item \textbf{Replay methods:} \\
    These approaches preserve a small memory with representative examples from the past or use generative models to recreate synthetic data. 
    During the training of new tasks, the network periodically revisits this memory to keep previous information active. 
    Examples of such techniques include Incremental Classifier and Representation Learning (iCaRL) and Generative Replay based on Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) or Variational Autoencoders (VAEs).

    \item \textbf{Parameter isolation or partitioning methods:} \\
    Instead of reusing the same weights for all tasks, these methods assign subsets of neurons or specific layers to each new task, reducing interference between them. 
    Models such as Progressive Neural Networks or PackNet gradually expand the architecture, preserving learned connections and adding new capacity without modifying existing ones. 
    This approach is useful in contexts where the number of tasks is limited or known in advance.

    \item \textbf{Distillation and knowledge transfer methods:} \\
    Inspired by Knowledge Distillation, these models transfer information from a trained network (“teacher”) to a new network (“student”), seeking to preserve learned internal representations. 
    During incremental learning, the student network learns the new tasks while attempting to replicate the teacher’s predictions for previous tasks, thus reducing the degree of forgetting.

    \item \textbf{Hybrid or multi-strategy methods:} \\
    In the most recent systems, the above strategies are combined to achieve a more efficient balance between stability and plasticity. 
    These models integrate regularisation, selective replay, and dynamic architectural expansion, adapting their behaviour according to the nature of the data or tasks. 
    Examples include Dynamic Architecture Networks or Meta-Incremental Learning schemes, where the system learns to adjust its own consolidation mechanism.
\end{enumerate}

Each of these approaches seeks, to some extent, to emulate human cognitive processes of learning and retention. 
However, most require manual control of model complexity or partial storage of old data, which limits their scalability in real-world environments. 

In this context, the model proposed by \cite{bullinaria2009} represents a biologically inspired alternative that avoids the use of explicit memories or external structures, instead implementing a dual-memory system through duplication of synaptic weights. 
In this way, new knowledge is gradually consolidated, reproducing the process of information transfer from the hippocampus to the neocortex in the human brain. 

The present work takes up this idea and proposes its extension, assessing whether the use of multiple weight duplications and different levels of consolidation can offer a more robust and scalable solution to catastrophic forgetting in incremental learning.
